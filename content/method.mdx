<Section id="shape" n="00" title="The shape of it">

Every project in this archive was built inside the same machine: Claude Code, wrapped in a harness that took longer to sharpen than most of the projects it has shipped. A standing contract that makes the model argue with me. Seventeen slash-skills that turn vague intent into a repeatable pipeline. A rim of hooks that guard the loop deterministically. Two specialist subagents that do the heavy reading in their own context windows. And a meter that prices every conversation after the fact.

The premise is the same one running inside my products: **probabilistic imagination, deterministic execution.** The harness is where I apply my own doctrine to myself.

<Section id="contract" n="01" title="The contract">

The global CLAUDE.md is four sentences of posture, and it changes everything downstream. *Treat my framing as a hypothesis, not a spec.* *Pressure-test consequential decisions.* *Disagree out loud — prioritize correctness over agreement.* *Decide vs. ask: resolve trivial choices yourself, surface the ones that matter.*

Most people prompt for compliance and get it, which is the problem. The contract makes the default posture adversarial: the model's job is the best outcome, not fast agreement. Every skill below inherits this — the critic exists because of it, and the challenge step assumes it.

</Section>

<Section id="loop" n="02" title="The loop">

Ideate, plan, build, challenge, wrap. Each stage is a skill with a sharp shape, and the sequence is the same whether the product is a grocery agent or this website.

The plan rules are worth quoting because they encode a worldview: **no time estimates, ever** — they're noise. **Phase boundaries are checkpoints, not chapters** — each ends exactly where I should verify something works, or where the model needs my hands. A plan that can't name its checkpoints isn't a plan; it's a vibe with numbered headings.

</Section>

<Section id="context" n="03" title="Context is the scarce resource">

The main context window is the most expensive real estate in the system, and almost everything in the harness exists to protect it.

Research is delegated to a researcher subagent that reads the web in its own window and returns cited conclusions — the brief it receives is self-contained because it cannot see the conversation. Codebase recon goes to Explore agents that return the map, not the territory. The critic reads code read-only in its own window and sends back ranked concerns. The main loop stays small, opinionated, and cheap to keep coherent.

Between sessions, the same discipline: `/handoff` writes a timestamped file — state, decisions with their *why*, ordered next steps — and a SessionStart hook injects just the git state and that file's next-steps section into the new session's first tokens. `/onboard` then loads the handoff and **verifies its claims against reality before trusting them**, flagging drift between what was written and what the repo says. Continuity without dragging a thousand turns of history along.

</Section>

<Section id="rim" n="04" title="The deterministic rim">

Four hooks wrap the loop — deterministic shell, no model, and they fail open, because they guard accidents, not deliberate obfuscation.

The bash guard is the interesting one: it blocks a recursive delete when the target *resolves* to a protected directory or an **ancestor** of one, while anything strictly inside stays deletable — a project's `dist/` dies freely, the dev root never does. The secrets guard refuses to write what looks like a real key into a tracked file, while `.env` and example files stay exempt. Format-on-edit runs ruff or prettier or gofmt after every write, silently. And the session-context hook keeps the boot prefix lean on purpose — orientation in a dozen lines, not a wall.

The same shape as my products' gates: small, pure, auditable, and positioned where the blast radius lives.

</Section>

<Section id="deep" n="05" title="Paying for certainty">

Every light skill has a heavyweight sibling, and the split is an economic decision. `/challenge` sends one Opus critic; `/deep-challenge` runs a **panel** — five lenses (correctness, failure modes, security, scale, simpler alternatives), one to three independent critics per lens depending on an intensity dial, merged, deduped, then steelmanned and ranked by an Opus pass. `/plan` sends one scout; `/deep-plan` fans four to eight explorers in parallel, drafts **three competing designs** — MVP-first, risk-first, robustness-first — scores them with a judge, and grafts the losers' best ideas onto the winner.

The deep family is deliberately *not* an agent swarm. The skill frames a self-contained brief; a co-located `workflow.js` executes a scripted, bounded fan-out. Imagination chooses the question; the script guarantees the coverage.

Certainty has a price, and the dial makes it explicit: spend a few hundred thousand tokens of parallel scrutiny on decisions that are expensive to be wrong about, and nothing on the ones that aren't.

</Section>

<Section id="meter" n="06" title="The meter">

You can't claim "build fast, adapt faster" without knowing what fast costs. Six token-accounting skills read the on-disk transcripts — main thread plus every subagent window, including everything long since evicted from live context — and split usage into fresh input, cache writes, cache reads, and output, per thread, with peak context per window.

One lens prices the current session. One prices just the last skill invocation. Two reach back into any past session without re-ingesting it. Two run a census across *all* sessions and skills — a ranked leaderboard of where the tokens actually went, with grand totals, written to a ledger. Each report opens as an HTML drill-down where every subagent row expands to the exact prompt it was fed and what it read, ran, and fetched.

The meter is also honest about itself: the dollar figures are API-list-price equivalents — a compute yardstick, not the bill — and input counts under-report, so trust output and cache-read. The point isn't accounting theater. It's that **isolation is verifiable**: the census shows the main window staying small while subagents process the bulk of the tokens in their own windows — the context-protection claim from section 03, as a number.

</Section>

<Section id="gate" n="07" title="Verification without me">

The highest-leverage question in the whole system is asked *before* any task starts: **can the agent verify this without me?** If the answer is no — no test to run, no eval case, no seeded data — that gets built first. It feels slower; it's faster by mid-session, because the agent self-corrects instead of queueing on my eyeballs. Every loop where a human must look at the output before the agent can continue is the bottleneck, and this question is how those loops get found and deleted.

The convention that enforces it: every focus repo carries an executable `.claude/gate.sh` — lint plus the full hermetic test suite, target under 120 seconds, 2–13 seconds in practice. A global guard-commit hook runs it on **every `git commit`** and blocks on red; `--no-verify` is the deliberate escape hatch, said out loud in the message. One file, three payoffs: agents self-check before committing, I stop reviewing broken diffs, and CI can run the same script the day a repo gets a remote.

Evals are the same idea for the nondeterministic parts. Anything with an LLM decision gets an offline, zero-credential `.claude/evals.sh` — JIM's runs 88 cases in about two seconds for $0.00, armed with a baseline so a regression is a verdict, not a feeling. And the whole thing runs while I sleep: a LaunchAgent fires at 06:17, runs every repo's gate and evals, and writes a dated digest. The morning session banner reads it; so does this site.

<Section id="lanes" n="08" title="Two lanes, then five">

The session shape that changes throughput isn't a better prompt — it's **conduct, don't type**. One main session plans and reviews; implementation runs in background agents and git worktrees. Serial agentic development is single-threading a multicore machine.

What makes parallel lanes cheap instead of chaotic is the lane contract. Every lane starts with a **written brief**: the goal, the constraints, the files to respect, the exact verify command it must pass — usually that repo's `gate.sh` — and what "done" means. If I can't write the brief, the work isn't ready to delegate; it gets planned in the conductor first. Lanes verify themselves against their gates, so I review *diffs*, not progress. And a lane gets touched only when it pings or at its checkpoint — watching a stream is the anti-pattern the whole setup exists to eliminate.

The ramp is deliberately boring: one conductor plus one executor, adding a lane only when the current count feels idle. The ceiling isn't the model — it's brief-writing and diff-review bandwidth, which plateaus usefully around three to five lanes. If lanes are frequently blocked on me, the diagnosis is too many lanes or too-vague briefs, never "the agent is slow."

</Section>

<Section id="flywheel" n="09" title="The flywheel">

Everything above compounds through one weekly ritual and one rule.

The rule: **anything done or explained three times becomes a skill that session** — a project skill in the repo, global if it's universal. The ritual: a thirty-minute weekly retro over one question — *where did I babysit this week?* Every intervention logged during the week (one line in a babysit file, ten seconds) gets converted to the cheapest permanent fix, in strict order: a hook, then a gate line, then an instruction line, then a skill, then — sometimes — acceptance. Skip the retro for a month and the setup stays exactly as good as it is today; run it weekly and every annoyance is felt approximately once.

Instruction files are part of the same loop, treated as code: one canonical `AGENTS.md` per repo (CLAUDE.md just points at it, so any harness reads the same contract), containing only what an agent gets wrong without being told — run commands, invariants, do-not lists, current phase. Every re-explanation in a session is a bug in that file, fixed with one line, then, not someday.

The proof this flywheel is real is the page you're reading it on. This site watches the fleet's transcripts and repos, reads the nightly digests, **files its own report every morning** — cut, sanitized in code, committed, pushed, redeployed, no hands — and once a week checks every project entry against its source repo and syncs the drift, gated by its own content check and build rather than by my attention. The archive maintains itself and shows its work in <a href="/v2/ops">the ops room</a>: the night watch rows, each agent's gate line, the curator's own sessions on the board.

---

The harness is why a one-person shop ships agents on payment rails with red-team suites. Same nervous system as the products, pointed at the producer — and increasingly, running without him.
